To play better **as a wolf in Werewolf**, keep track of three things: what the village knows, what you have already said and what still helps your team. Join discussions, give reasons for your votes and avoid automatically defending your teammates. No bluff guarantees a win, but consistent reasoning is easier to maintain than a new story every round.

This guide focuses on the wolves. If you are trying to uncover the pack, read our [Villager strategy guide](/en/blog/werewolf-villager-role-strategy/).

## Understand the information you start with

In the game described by [Stellar Factory](https://playwerewolf.co/pages/rules), wolves recognise each other at night and choose a victim together. Variants can change this setup, so check your game’s actions and winning conditions.

You may know something the other players are still trying to discover. Before speaking, ask yourself: **“Could a villager work this out from the public discussion?”** Declaring someone innocent too quickly, without explaining why, can draw attention.

Check our [role guide](/en/blog/werewolf-roles-explained/) if a character is unfamiliar. Understanding the powers before roles are assigned is more useful than asking what an opponent does halfway through the night.

## Join the discussion without inventing an entire investigation

You do not have to accuse someone every time you speak. Asking for clarification, recalling a vote or comparing two explanations lets you take part in the apparent search for wolves.

Use events that actually happened in the game: “You suspected Leah, then voted for Sam. What changed?” That question concerns something others can check. You can respond to the answer without maintaining a made-up memory.

Leave room for uncertainty. “I lean towards Sam because of that vote” is easier to explain than absolute certainty with no public evidence behind it. Speak at your usual pace; staying completely silent or dominating every exchange can both make the discussion harder.

## Support a teammate without changing your standards

Defending another wolf against every accusation can link the two of you. Accusing them every round to look independent can cost your team a player. Judge the situation instead of making either approach an automatic rule.

**An invented example:** you and Noah are wolves. On the first day, you criticised Alex for voting without giving a reason. The next day, Camille makes exactly that criticism of Noah.

Saying “votes don’t need explanations” would contradict your argument from the previous day. You could ask Noah, “What made you choose Alex?”, then discuss his answer. You keep the same standard without pretending his innocence is established. That will not necessarily save Noah, but it avoids adding a contradiction to the accusation.

If you genuinely change your mind about a target, name the part of the discussion that changed it. The group may still doubt you, but your vote has an identifiable explanation.

## Choose a night target with the next day in mind

Someone accusing you does not automatically make them the priority. When comparing targets allowed by your rules, consider:

- Who connects the votes and helps the village decide?
- Who provides credible information about roles?
- Whom is the group likely to listen to in the next discussion?
- Which known or possible ability could change the night’s outcome?

For example, Claire summarises contradictions and persuades other players, while Malik accuses you without convincing anyone. Claire may be the greater threat even if Malik annoys you more. That is a way to weigh the choice, not a target that is always correct.

Protection and other powers can disrupt your plan. A night with no elimination does not, by itself, reveal everybody’s role. Use the information actually announced by the moderator or app.

## Stay focused when suspicion falls on you

Answer the specific accusation first: a vote, a statement or a change of mind. Immediately accusing the person who questioned you can leave their original question unanswered.

Bluffing belongs in the game. Personal attacks, secret private messages and peeking at another player’s role do not help the deduction. Use only the communication allowed by your rules and follow the instructions for eliminated players.

After the game, ask what gave you away. A contradiction in your votes gives you something concrete to work on; losing does not mean every decision was wrong.
