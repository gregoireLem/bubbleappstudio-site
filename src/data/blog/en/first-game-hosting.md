When **hosting Werewolf for first-time players**, plan how they will join the conversation. Explain the goal, demonstrate voting before dealing roles, and make room for questions without asking anyone to disclose their character. This guide covers welcoming players and managing discussion; use your [game’s rules](/en/blog/how-to-play-werewolf/) for powers and victory conditions.

Once the instructions are clear, choose a welcome from our [funny narrator lines](/en/blog/funny-werewolf-narrator-lines/). Keep explanations and role calls separate from the jokes.

## Before dealing roles, show players what they will do

Arrange seats so everyone can see and hear each other. Ask who is new without treating that answer as their identity for the whole evening. Someone may prefer to watch the first game; settle that before counting player roles.

Introduce the two teams and the cycle of night, discussion and voting. Explain only the powers in your [chosen setup](/en/blog/werewolf-group-role-setup/). Cover ties and what eliminated players may do under your version’s rules.

Next, run a **practice vote with no roles assigned and nothing at stake**. For example, have the group choose a fictional village name using the gesture or procedure they will use in the game. Make clear that nobody is eliminated. The point is to show when a choice becomes final, not to test anyone’s bluffing.

Hosting over the holidays? Our [Christmas family games guide](/en/blog/christmas-family-games/) has two games people can dip into while dinner is being cleared, plus a way to introduce Werewolf once everyone settles in.

## Give the first discussion somewhere to start

On the first day, there may be little public information. Do not promise a clue that the rules have not provided. Offer an optional opening round: each person can ask a question, share an observation or say “I don’t have a view yet.”

Introduce these prompts before play begins:

- “What made you choose that person?”
- “You changed your mind. Which argument persuaded you?”
- “For now, I suspect this person for this reason, but I could be wrong.”

These are conversation tools, not evidence. Hesitation or confusion about a rule does not reveal someone’s team. Our [Villager guide](/en/blog/werewolf-villager-role-strategy/) explains how to separate observations from suspicions.

## An example of sharing the floor

Imagine Jordan accuses Sam and two friends answer before Sam can speak. The host can say, “Let Sam finish, then we’ll hear your responses.” That protects a turn without supporting or rejecting the accusation.

If Sam says “I don’t know what to say,” try an open question: “What stood out to you in the discussion?” Avoid “You’re a Villager, defend yourself!” That wording assumes a role. Neutral language matters especially when a human moderator knows everyone’s identities.

Agree on the discussion format beforehand. Any time limit should fit your rules; do not suddenly cut the debate short because an accusation sounds convincing.

## Handle rules questions without exposing a role

Before roles are assigned, explain how players can ask for clarification. With a human moderator, arrange a discreet way to ask. With an app, follow the available instructions without showing a secret screen to neighbouring players.

A general question can become a reminder for everyone: “Let’s clarify how this ability works in our version.” Do not follow up by asking who has that role. If an explanation could expose information, use the agreed way to handle it rather than inventing a public ruling on the spot.
