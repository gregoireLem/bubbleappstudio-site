Looking for a game like Undercover to play with friends? **Werewolf is worth considering if you enjoy defending a secret team and following other players’ decisions.** To choose, ask your group what they would rather examine: clues about words, or the accusations and votes that build up during a game.

Here, Undercover means [Yanstar Studio’s word game](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/undercover-word-party-game/id946882449). Werewolf Undercover is our Werewolf app, published by Bubble App Studio. They are separate apps. This guide offers our suggestions for choosing an in-person activity.

## The differences that matter at the table

| Your preference | Undercover | Werewolf |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clues to interpret | The words people use in their descriptions | Stated opinions, votes and information revealed during play |
| A starting point for speaking | An idea connected to your word | A question or an accusation you can explain |
| What to introduce | The idea of secret words | The teams, night/day cycle and selected roles |

Use this comparison to choose an experience. It is not a ranking, and it does not assume matching play times or features across versions.

## Undercover: listen to how people describe things

In [Yanstar Studio’s rules](https://www.yanstarstudio.com/undercover-how-to-play), Civilians share a word, the Undercover receives a related word and Mr. White receives none. Players describe, discuss and then vote.

Here is an invented example of that kind of discussion, rather than a pair taken from the app’s word list: imagine “lantern” and “flashlight.” The clue “light” could fit either. “Hanging” raises a more specific question: is that person thinking of the same object as you?

The interesting part is comparing interpretations. An unusual description does not automatically prove anything. A player might have a different experience of an object, be searching for vocabulary or hesitate to give too much away.

Before choosing this format, consider whether everyone feels comfortable with the language and word associations. A practice example with nothing at stake can help you separate a vocabulary difficulty from a bluff.

## Werewolf: follow decisions across the game

In the classic format covered by our [rules guide](/en/blog/how-to-play-werewolf/), secret night actions alternate with village discussions and voting. The roles included determine which information and powers are available.

Now imagine this scene: Mia defends Ben, then votes against him after Sam speaks, without explaining what changed her mind. A useful question becomes: “What information made you change your vote?” You are comparing a decision with an earlier explanation. It does not prove Mia’s team; it gives the group something specific to examine.

We would suggest this format to friends who enjoy revisiting a conversation, comparing accounts and watching a situation develop. Before starting, explain only the roles being used and agree on how eliminated players and tied votes will be handled.

## Three questions to choose tonight’s game

- **What do you enjoy about bluffing?** Finding a phrase with two possible meanings points towards wordplay; defending a decision and questioning alliances points towards Werewolf.
- **How does each person like to join in?** Let everyone try one practice sentence. A quiet player can make a useful observation without wanting to deliver a speech.
- **Can everyone stay for the whole game?** If friends are arriving, leaving or serving dinner, wait until the group can stay involved. Agree on when to take breaks.

Avoid basing the choice entirely on the most experienced player’s confidence. Ask newcomers what they have understood and what they would like to try, too.

## Switching games without mixing up the rules

Both games can appeal to people who enjoy suspicion and discussion. When you switch, explain the voting procedure, what information gets revealed and the win conditions again.
