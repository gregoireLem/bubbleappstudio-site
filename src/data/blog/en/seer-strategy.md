The Seer is usually a village role that receives secret information about a player at night. That information can help the village vote more accurately. The challenge is choosing a useful check and sharing the result without becoming an obvious target too early.

This guide focuses on an in-person game with several nights and builds on our [Werewolf roles overview](/en/blog/werewolf-roles-explained/). The examples assume reliable results and roles that stay the same throughout the game. Adjust the advice if your version includes powers that change either of those conditions.

## What does the Seer actually learn?

It depends on the game. For The Werewolves of Millers Hollow, [Asmodee describes seeing a player’s card](https://www.asmodee.fr/les-roles-des-loups-garous-de-thiercelieux-que-vous-allez-adorer/). In Ultimate Werewolf, [Bezier Games describes learning whether a player is a Werewolf](https://beziergames.com/products/ultimate-werewolf). An exact role and a “wolf or not” result are different kinds of information.

Before playing, check what your result means, when you act, and whether players may claim roles during discussion. If you use an app, follow its role description. Do not turn “not a wolf” into “ordinary Villager” unless the rules actually give you that detail.

## Who should the Seer check at night?

Look for information that could change the next vote. Ask yourself: “What would I do tomorrow if this person is a wolf? What if they are not?” If neither answer changes your decision, another player may be a more useful target.

- **Someone at the centre of a disagreement** may help you assess two competing accounts.
- **An influential player** can be worth checking because their arguments shape several votes. Confidence alone does not identify their team.
- **Someone you have not checked yet** may tell you more than a repeat check, provided no power can change roles or alter results.

On the first night, you may have little evidence. Checking someone whose arguments you expect to find difficult to assess is a reasonable starting point. Being quiet, laughing or looking nervous is not proof on its own.

## An example across two nights

On night one, you check Ana and learn that she is not a wolf. The following day, Ben and Clara accuse each other. Ana may still be wrong about her own suspects: your result does not make all of her opinions correct.

On night two, you check Clara because her accusation is shaping the discussion. The result says “wolf.” You now have two separate results. Neither establishes that Ben is innocent; two wolves can accuse each other too.

Remember three details for every check: **the night, the player and the exact result**. Keep those separate from your deductions about everyone else. If your game allows team changes or misleading results, consider those rules before treating an earlier result as certain information about the present.

## When should you reveal that you are the Seer?

If role claims are allowed, weigh the value of speaking now against the value of another night. Waiting may let you gather more information, but waiting too long can mean being eliminated before sharing it.

Speaking up becomes especially useful when you are about to be voted out, when a wolf you identified is likely to escape the vote, or when your result could prevent a decisive mistake by the village. None of those situations guarantees that others will believe you.

In the example, a clear claim would be: “I am the Seer. Night one, Ana: not a wolf. Night two, Clara: wolf. I suggest voting for Clara.” Do not add “so Ben must be innocent.” Respect your table’s rules about showing secret cards or screens, even when you want to support your claim.

## Three mistakes to avoid

Do not assume someone is innocent just because they believe you. Avoid accusing the whole table at once: explain your result first, then your proposed vote. Finally, do not assume that revealing your role guarantees protection. Check which powers are in play and what their limits are.
