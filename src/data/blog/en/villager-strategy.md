To play Villager well in Werewolf, **remember a specific action, ask for the reason behind it and compare the answer with earlier discussion**. You do not need a night power to help the village choose a vote. Your job is to give everyone something concrete to examine.

This guide covers the ordinary Villager with no special ability in an in-person game. The village team can also include characters with powers. Our [roles guide](/en/blog/werewolf-roles-explained/) explains the difference.

## What does a regular Villager do?

Under common rules, a Villager works to have the Werewolves eliminated through the village vote. At night, they keep their eyes closed and take no special action. By day, they join the discussion and vote while still in the game. Follow your version’s instructions for eliminations and win conditions.

[Stellar Factory’s character guide](https://playwerewolf.co/pages/character-roles) likewise describes the Villager as investigating through conversation. The advice and example below are our suggestions for beginners.

Your own role is private information you know. Hearing someone else say “I’m a Villager” does not confirm their role: a Werewolf can make the same claim.

## Separate an observation, a theory and a question

Before accusing someone, try stating these three things:

- **Observation:** “You said you would vote for Ben, then chose Mia.” The group can check whether that happened.
- **Theory:** “You might be trying to protect Ben.” That is a possibility, not knowledge of a role.
- **Question:** “What made you change your mind?” This gives you an explanation to consider before deciding.

Keeping these separate also lets you update your view without pretending you were certain all along. You can say: “The switch looked suspicious, but the explanation makes sense to me. I’ll look at another lead.”

## What can you say on day one with few clues?

Do not fill the silence with invented certainty. Ask someone for their current suspicion and what would change their mind. Then give another person room to contribute: one player asking every question can crowd out the discussion.

A shy player may hesitate; an experienced player may talk a lot. Neither behavior identifies a team on its own. Focus on a decision and its explanation, and give beginners time to understand the question.

For the order of a typical day, see our [Werewolf rules guide](/en/blog/how-to-play-werewolf/).

## Example: should you follow Alex’s accusation?

Imagine this discussion before the vote:

1. Alex says they will vote for Ben because Ben supposedly switched accusations without a reason.
2. Mia asks Alex to repeat exactly what Ben said.
3. Alex now says they will vote for Mia: “She asks too many questions.” No new information about Ben has appeared between those statements.

You have observed a change of target, not identified a Werewolf. A useful question would be: “Alex, does your reason for suspecting Ben still stand? Why does Mia’s question matter more?” Hear the answer before weighing this suspicion against the others.

Now suppose Mia is eliminated and your variant reveals eliminated roles: she was a Villager. That gives you a reason to revisit the arguments against her. It does not automatically make Alex a Werewolf; Villagers can be mistaken too. If roles stay hidden in your game, you do not have that confirmation.

## How should you defend yourself?

Answer the specific point raised. “I changed targets after Ben’s explanation” gives the group more to discuss than repeatedly saying “I swear I’m innocent.” Explain the reason you had at the time and whether you still believe it.

For a first game, avoid inventing a power or a Seer result to save yourself. Other Villagers may treat your story as real information. Showing how you reached a decision can help even when you cannot prove your role.

## Check three things before voting

- Can I name something that happened in this game to support my choice?
- Have I listened to the accused player’s answer?
- Why is this suspicion stronger than the other lead being discussed?

Give your reason briefly and accept that it could be wrong. Reconsider your theories after the result. If you are eliminated and your rules require silence, save your comments for the debrief: helping your team does not allow hints from the sidelines.
